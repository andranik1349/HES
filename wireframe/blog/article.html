What MML-1 actually means — and why most frameworks don't have one

Areg Oganesian · 8 min read Read on Substack ↗

Most psychological frameworks describe their constructs as if they exist and can be measured, without distinguishing between those with decades of robust validation and those that remain theoretical sketches. The Human Elements System takes a different approach: every element carries an MML rating — a Measurement Maturity Level — that makes this distinction explicit.

MML-1, the highest level, is deliberately hard to achieve. It requires not just one good study, but multiple independent validation studies with consistent results, conducted by different research groups, using different methodologies, across different populations. Most psychological constructs — even well-known ones — do not qualify.

The infrastructure question

The key phrase in the MML-1 definition is "measurement infrastructure." This means the field has agreed-upon operationalisations, standardised tasks or questionnaires with published psychometric properties, and norms for interpretation. Without this infrastructure, claims about a construct's prevalence or predictive validity cannot be meaningfully compared across studies.

Consider C003Working Memory Capacity — one of the clearest examples of an MML-1 element in the HES Core layer. N-back variants, reading span, and operation span tasks have been validated across hundreds of independent studies. The Baddeley & Hitch multi-component model provides a stable theoretical framework. Cross-cultural replications exist. This is what measurement maturity looks like.

Note: MML ratings in HES are assigned at the time of last revision and are updated when new evidence warrants. An element's MML reflects the state of the published literature, not the author's confidence in the construct's existence.

Why cognitive load is harder to pin down

Compare this with D012Cognitive Load Sensitivity — a Derived element that captures individual differences in susceptibility to performance degradation under load. While cognitive load theory as a pedagogical framework is well-developed, the individual differences construct — stable variation in how sensitive people are to load — sits at MML-2. Measurement is possible but cross-study replication of the individual-differences component specifically is limited.

This is the kind of distinction HES is designed to surface. Both constructs are real and important. But treating them as equally validated in a research design would be a mistake that an explicit maturity framework prevents.

What the scale implies for practitioners

The MML scale is not a quality judgment about which constructs matter more. An MML-4 element can be theoretically central and practically significant — it simply hasn't yet accumulated the validation evidence needed for a higher rating. The rating tells you what kind of epistemic claim you are making when you use the element in applied work, and what the literature can and cannot yet support.

For practitioners, this means MML-1 and MML-2 elements are appropriate for use in assessment contexts with reasonable confidence. MML-3 elements warrant caution and should be treated as indicative rather than definitive. MML-4 and MML-5 elements are included in HES for theoretical completeness — they map the landscape, not the terrain that has been surveyed.
